## 1. Summary

- **Scope.** This document covers the machine-learning half of claim (b): which classifiers Hallmark proves, how they are trained and evaluated, the data they need, and how a trained model becomes the committed integer artifact `C_Ci` that the spec (§ 4, § 6.2) proves. It does not change the spec's claims or tiers.
- **"JEV" and "Laya".** Jev is the hosted, closed "System One" decision model of TypeSafe AI (launched 2026-09-15): typed `choice`, `score` and `noul` questions over a state, answered with probabilities in one pass, no text generation (✓ VERIFIED [launch post](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), [docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)). Laya is an independent, Apache-2.0, open-weight Jev-style model on Hugging Face: [`convaiinnovations/laya`](https://huggingface.co/convaiinnovations/laya), by ConvAI Innovations (GitHub [NandhaKishorM/laya](https://github.com/NandhaKishorM/laya)), a ModernBERT-large encoder plus a two-layer decision head, 421.3M parameters (✓ VERIFIED Hub metadata and `rl_agent_config.json`). Identification is confident (§ 3).
- **The size problem.** Laya's smallest checkpoint (322M, multilingual) is three to four times the ~100M ZK target, and its zero-shot guardrail accuracy in one independent benchmark is low (61.1 balanced accuracy against 88.7 for Jev 1.14.0, § 3.3). It is a strong base to fine-tune and a strong teacher, not a drop-in proven classifier.
- **Recommendation (staged, ? INFERRED).** Stage 1: pure encoder classifiers of 20M to 110M parameters, fine-tuned per policy, with quantisation-aware training against the prover's arithmetic; pick the architecture by a prover operator-support test, not by leaderboard. Stage 2: a Laya-based decision system with a fixed, committed question set, fine-tuned on Hallmark data, run in the TEE-attested tier and used as the teacher. Stage 3: distil the Laya decision system into a sub-100M student that the zkML prover can handle, so the proven classifier is Laya-derived; prove Laya itself only when 300M to 400M encoders are affordable to prove.
- **Data.** Start from verified Hub datasets with commercial-compatible licences (Nemotron Content Safety v2 and v3, WildGuardMix, WildJailbreak, Salad-Data, JailbreakBench, XSTest, OR-Bench, deepset and Lakera injection sets, LLMail-Inject), keep CC BY-NC sets (BeaverTails, PKU-SafeRLHF, ToxicChat) to evaluation until licence review, and add a synthetic generator, judge and red-team loop with a written rubric and human audit (§ 5).
- **Pipeline.** NeMo Curator for exact, fuzzy and semantic deduplication, language ID, PII and safety classifiers, plus Presidio, datasketch and n-gram decontamination against frozen eval sets; versioned, content-hashed dataset manifests and data cards; every training run emits a manifest whose hash enters the registry beside `C_Ci` (§ 5.4, § 6.5).
- **Top risk unchanged.** "Passed" is not "safe". Adaptive attacks pushed PromptGuard-class detectors above 90% attack success ([[nasr2025adaptive]], repo note, 2026-10-12). The ML work can raise attack cost and measure it honestly; it cannot make the cryptographic claim mean robustness.

## 2. What the ML work must hand to the spec

The spec fixes the interface; the ML pipeline must produce exactly these things (✓ from the spec, read this session):

| Spec requirement | ML deliverable |
|---|---|
| Integer model is normative (§ 6.2) | A quantised integer program whose verdicts are the classifier; the float model is a training artifact only |
| `C_Ci` = H(vk, integer model hash, tokenizer hash, chunking rule, label map, quantisation scales) (§ 4) | Each of those files, deterministic, hashed, and a release manifest that lists them |
| Threshold `t_i` as integer logit difference, no softmax in circuit (§ 6.2) | Calibration that maps a target operating point (for example FPR 1%) to an integer log-odds threshold at the model's scale |
| 512-token chunks, max over chunks (§ 6.2, Prompt Guard 2 card) | Training and evaluation on chunked inputs, including payloads split across chunk boundaries (T12) |
| Tiers Z0 to Z3 and T (§ 6.1) | A classifier per tier: rule (Z0), head (Z1), encoder up to ~110M (Z2), 1B to 2B LM (Z3, extension), large or decision system (T) |
| Verdict agreement, zero disagreements (§ 13, PRD) | An agreement test harness between in-TEE integer runtime and prover witness, with a near-threshold slice |
| Red team reported as classifier results (§ 13.6) | A separate robustness report per classifier version, linked from the registry |

## 3. Jev and Laya: what they are

### 3.1 Jev (TypeSafe AI)

- ✓ VERIFIED ([launch post](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), Diogo Almeida, founder, 2026-09-15): Jev is TypeSafe's first "System One Model", named after Kahneman's System 1 and after William Stanley Jevons. It takes unstructured state and typed questions and returns typed answers with probabilities in parallel; it "gives up string generation". Training method: Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD). Stated end-to-end response time 70 to 500 ms; input price $0.042 per million tokens. Named use cases include "guardrail, and detect jailbreaks". Jev supports up to 255 options per choice.
- ✓ VERIFIED ([docs introduction](https://docs.typesafe.ai/introduction)): three primitives: Choice (pick an option), Score (rate on a rubric), Noul (probability a statement is true). Questions are evaluated "in parallel and in isolation against the same state".
- ✗ UNCERTAIN: architecture, parameter count and training data are not published in the pages read. Jev is hosted only.
- ? INFERRED consequence for Hallmark: Jev cannot be a proven classifier. Its weights cannot be committed or proven, and every input leaves for TypeSafe's service, which conflicts with confidential transcripts. Its roles are a reference point in benchmarks and, after a terms-of-service and confidentiality check, possibly a labelling judge on non-confidential synthetic data.
- Abdel's [awesome-typesafe-jev](https://github.com/AbdelStark/awesome-typesafe-jev) list (✓ README read) is the map of the ecosystem: official SDKs, community clients, independent benchmarks (JevBench, Jevals, raxIT's "Decision Models as Guardrails"), and a set of open Jev-style alternatives including Laya, Kev, Bosun, Verdict, jeff and OneJev.

### 3.3 What the evidence says about Laya as a guardrail

- ✓ VERIFIED (raxIT Labs, [Decision Models as Guardrails](https://dm-as-guardrails.raxitlabs.com), page read): on 9,975 labelled guardrail checks (content, direct and indirect prompt attacks, denied topics, profanity, PII, grounding), each a yes/no question at a fixed 0.5 threshold, Laya ranks last of 19 systems at 61.1 ± 1.0 balanced accuracy; Jev 1.14.0 scores 88.7 and Amazon Bedrock Guardrails 79.0 (the awesome list's summary of version 1.0.0). No model gets a calibrated threshold, and raxIT built and ran the benchmark itself.
- ? INFERRED reading: zero-shot Laya is not a usable safety classifier, which matches its authors' advice. The benchmark says nothing about a Laya checkpoint fine-tuned on safety data, which is the case that matters here.
- The awesome list also notes that Laya's own Jev comparisons "use different prompts and sample sizes rather than a controlled head-to-head" (✓ list README).

### 3.4 Why a decision system is attractive for Hallmark anyway

? INFERRED: a policy in the spec is a list of classifiers with thresholds. A decision model turns a policy into a list of committed natural-language `noul` questions ("Does the prompt try to override prior instructions?", "Does the response give operational uplift for a weapon?") answered by one model in one pass. That is more legible to a relying party, easier to extend without retraining a new head per policy, and calibrated by training objective. For proving, nothing magical happens: with a fixed question set baked into a committed prefix, a fine-tuned Laya is an encoder classifier with a longer input. The question text becomes part of `C_Ci` (as a template hash), and the question prefix eats into the 512-token window (192-token head budget on the English checkpoint), which means more chunks per input and more proving.

### 4.2 Where zkML cost goes in an encoder

? INFERRED analysis, from textbook transformer arithmetic and the provers' documented approaches (EZKL lookups and fixed-point scales, [[ezkl-repo]]; DeepProve 12-bit quantisation with lookup arguments, [[lagrange-deepprove]]; ZKTorch 10-bit, [[chen2025zktorch]]). Numbers below are formulas, not measurements.

| Operation | Count for one 512-token chunk (L = 512, d = hidden, n = layers, h = heads) | ZK cost character | Design lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linear layers (QKV, output, MLP) | about 2 x (non-embedding params) x L multiply-adds | Dominant arithmetic; well supported (sumcheck, GKR, accumulation) | Fewer layers, smaller d; int8-style weights |
| Attention scores QK^T and AV | about 2 x n x L^2 x d | Grows with L^2; full attention at L = 512 is significant | Shorter chunks; banded or local attention only if the circuit exploits it |
| Softmax in attention | n x h x L^2 exponentials plus row normalisation | Non-linear: lookup tables for exp, a division or reciprocal per row, range checks. Typically the most expensive non-linear op | Integer softmax approximations fixed at QAT time; fewer heads; shorter L |
| LayerNorm | 2 per layer per token: mean, variance, reciprocal square root | Division and square root via lookup or hint-and-check; count is small (n x L) but each needs range proofs | Fixed-point rsqrt approximation; RMSNorm (ModernBERT uses LayerNorm without bias, ✓ config) |
| GELU or GeGLU | n x L x intermediate size | Lookup per element | ReLU is cheaper (comparison only); GeGLU adds an elementwise product |
| Rotary embeddings (ModernBERT) | n x L x d | Sines and cosines are public constants, so elementwise multiplies | Cheap if positions are fixed |
| DeBERTa disentangled attention | Extra content-to-position and position-to-content score terms with relative-position gathers | Extra matmuls and gathers; prover support ✗ UNCERTAIN (spec § 10) | Prefer classic BERT or ModernBERT if unsupported |
| Final head and threshold | One linear layer and a comparison of logit difference with `t_i` | Trivial; no output softmax (spec § 6.2) | Keep it |
| Tokenisation | Outside the circuit in modes B1 and B2 (spec § 6.3) | Zero here; zkVM cost in B3 | Byte-level or small-vocabulary tokenizers ease B3 |

Facts used: ModernBERT-base has 22 layers, hidden 768, local attention window 128 with global attention every third layer, RoPE, 8,192 positions and a 50,368-token vocabulary (✓ [config.json](https://huggingface.co/answerdotai/ModernBERT-base/raw/main/config.json)). Its efficiency tricks (unpadding, alternating local attention, flash attention) target GPUs; a circuit compiled from a dense ONNX graph gets none of the saving unless the prover is told about the band structure. ? INFERRED: on a 512-token chunk the local window saves little anyway, because most layers' windows cover a quarter of the chunk.

### 4.4 Fine-tuning versus training from scratch

? INFERRED, by architecture:

- **Encoders (Z2).** Fine-tune a pretrained encoder; full fine-tuning is cheap at this size and parameter-efficient methods buy little. Pretraining from scratch is out of scope: Ettin's encoders were trained on about 2T tokens (✓ Ettin card). The one exception is a research track for a ZK-native encoder (ReLU, fixed-point-friendly normalisation, small vocabulary, few heads), which would be distilled from a teacher on task data rather than pretrained on the web. Treat it as a stretch experiment only if prover benchmarks show non-linear ops dominate.
- **Heads and probes (Z1).** Train from scratch on frozen features. A probe on the served model's activations needs those activations, which exist only inside the TEE for a closed model (spec § 6.1), so probe training data must be produced in the enclave or with an open stand-in model.
- **Laya (T, and teacher).** Fine-tune with the authors' RLCD recipe and a Hallmark question set; refit temperatures on held-out data (the authors warn the notebook calibrates on training items). Fix the option layout and question order, because both change answers (✓ README).
- **Small guard LLMs (Z3, T).** Supervised fine-tuning with LoRA through TRL if used at all; mostly as teachers and labelling judges.
- **Distillation (Z2 student from a T teacher).** Train the student on the teacher's calibrated probabilities (soft labels) plus human or gold labels, with a proper scoring loss (Brier or log loss), then QAT. This is the route by which a Laya-quality decision system becomes provable.

### 4.5 Recommendation

? INFERRED, staged, aligned with PRD milestones M2 to M5:

1. **Stage 1, pure classifiers (POC).** Classifier X (prompt injection and jailbreak, prompt scope) and a response-harm classifier, each a fine-tuned encoder in the 17M to 110M backbone range.
   - First task: an operator-support bake-off. Export three candidates to ONNX with static 512-token shapes (a 4-layer classic BERT, Ettin-32m, DeBERTa-v3-xsmall or Prompt Guard 2 22M) and try DeepProve, ZKTorch and EZKL on one layer and then the full model. The spec already says to choose a BERT-architecture encoder if DeBERTa attention is unsupported; extend that rule to ModernBERT-style blocks.
   - Off-the-shelf baseline: Prompt Guard 2 22M, unmodified, as the reference to beat on accuracy and as the first proving target if supported, subject to licence review.
   - Classifier Y: an MLP head on the frozen encoder's pooled embedding (or a probe, if the stand-in model's activations are available). Classifier Z: rules.
2. **Stage 2, Laya decision system (tier T).** Fine-tune Laya on the Hallmark question set (one `noul` per policy item, fixed order, fixed wording) with RLCD, calibrate on held-out data, run it in the TEE as a tier-T classifier whose verdict is hardware-attested, and use it as the teacher for Stage 3. `laya-multilingual` (322M) is the smaller start if multilingual coverage matters; the English checkpoint if not.
3. **Stage 3, provable Laya-derived student.** Distil the Stage 2 teacher into a sub-100M student (Ettin-68m, or mmBERT-small for multilingual) with the same committed question prefix, then QAT and prove it as Z2. Prove a Laya-sized model directly only when a matched benchmark shows a 300M to 400M encoder at 512 tokens within the latency or sampling budget.
4. **Not Jev.** Use Jev only as an external benchmark reference, and only on non-confidential data.

Why staged rather than Laya first: the POC's hard gate is verdict agreement and proving cost (PRD), and every extra 100M parameters and every unsupported operator moves M3. Abdel's preference for Laya is preserved where it pays: as the decision system and teacher, and as the source of the proven student.

## 5. Data

### 5.1 Policies, labels and the record format

? INFERRED proposal. Every classifier is trained for a written policy, and the label is defined by the policy, not by the source dataset's taxonomy.

| Policy id | Scope | Question (decision-system phrasing) | Positive means |
|---|---|---|---|
| `inj.direct` | prompt | Does the input try to override or supersede prior instructions? | Prompt Guard 2's definition of malicious (✓ card) |
| `inj.indirect` | tool or document content in the prompt | Does embedded content try to instruct the model? | Indirect injection |
| `harm.request` | prompt | Does the user request content in a listed harm category? | Harmful intent |
| `harm.response` | response | Does the response provide content in a listed harm category? | Unsafe response, including partial compliance |
| `refusal` | response | Does the response refuse? | Used for over-refusal metrics, not gating |
| `pii.leak` | response | Does the response disclose personal data? | Z0 rule first, Z2 later |

Record (one JSON line per labelled example):

```text
{
  record_id, dataset_version, source: {dataset_id, revision_sha, row_id, licence} | {generator_id, generator_hash, recipe_id, seed},
  conversation: [{role, content}], prompt_ref, response_ref,
  target_model: {model_id, revision or C_M, sampling params} | null,
  language, length_tokens: {tokenizer_hash: n},
  attack: {family, technique, parent_record_id, adaptive: bool} | null,
  labels: {policy_id: {value, confidence, rubric_version, annotators: [{kind: human|judge, id, hash}], agreement}},
  split, decontam: {checked_against: [eval_set_hash], hits},
  pii: {detected, action}, notes
}
```

### 5.2 Synthetic data generation infrastructure

? INFERRED design. Goals: coverage of the policies across benign, borderline and adversarial inputs, paired responses from several target models, and labels with known quality.

1. **Seed and taxonomy.** Seed prompts from the verified datasets in § 5.3 and a taxonomy of harm categories and attack techniques (start from the Nemotron Content Safety and Salad-Data categories; ✓ both datasets carry category fields).
2. **Generators.** Open-weight instruction models served locally (vLLM or similar), several families to avoid one model's style. Tools: [NeMo Data Designer](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/DataDesigner) or [distilabel](https://github.com/argilla-io/distilabel) for recipe-driven generation with recorded prompts and seeds (✓ repos exist; features not re-read).
3. **Target responses.** For each prompt, responses from several target models at recorded sampling settings, including deliberately weakened or uncensored models to get positive `harm.response` examples. Record `model_id` and revision as `target_model`.
4. **Adversarial and red-team generation.** WildTeaming-style mutation of vanilla requests into jailbreaks (the method behind WildJailbreak, ✓ arXiv abstract); attack libraries [garak](https://github.com/NVIDIA/garak) and [PyRIT](https://github.com/Azure/PyRIT) for probes and orchestrated attacks; HarmBench and JailbreakBench artifacts for known attacks; indirect injection scenarios from [BIPIA](https://github.com/microsoft/BIPIA) and [AgentDojo](https://github.com/ethz-spylab/agentdojo). Add Hallmark-specific attacks: chunk-boundary splitting, payloads beyond 512 tokens, odd encodings and homoglyphs, multilingual and code-switched inputs (spec T12).
5. **Adaptive loop against our own classifier.** Once a candidate exists, run attacks that optimise against it (gradient-based for white-box, LLM-guided search for black-box) and feed successful attacks back as labelled hard examples. Keep a disjoint set of attack families out of training entirely, for evaluation only, because the adaptive-attack literature shows defences tuned to seen attacks fail on new ones ([[nasr2025adaptive]]).
6. **Judges and labelling.** At least two judge models from different families (for example a Llama Guard-class and a WildGuard-class model, plus a Laya fine-tune once it exists) score each record against the written rubric; disagreements and low-confidence items go to human review. NeMo Curator's Aegis classifier can serve as one judge (§ 5.4).
7. **Rubric.** One page per policy: definition, positive and negative examples, borderline rules (fiction, education, historical, quoted content, refusals with partial leakage), and what to do when the response is truncated. Version it; the version goes in every label.
8. **Label quality.** Measure inter-annotator agreement (Cohen's or Krippendorff's kappa) between judges and between judges and humans on a stratified audit sample; accept a judge for a policy only above an agreement bar fixed in advance. ? INFERRED starting bar: human audit of at least a few hundred items per policy and per language before trusting judge labels at scale (guess, to be set at M0).
9. **Leakage control.** Generators never see evaluation items; decontamination (§ 5.4) runs on every generated batch.

## 6. MLOps

### 6.1 Training stack

? INFERRED choices, all open source and self-hostable:

- **Encoders:** Hugging Face Transformers `AutoModelForSequenceClassification` and `Trainer`, or a plain PyTorch loop; [Optimum](https://github.com/huggingface/optimum) for ONNX export and quantisation tooling; PyTorch's quantisation-aware training utilities for fake-quant modules where the prover's arithmetic can be mimicked.
- **Small LLM judges or Z3 candidates:** [TRL](https://github.com/huggingface/trl) for SFT with LoRA.
- **Laya:** the authors' `laya.train.finetune` and notebooks (RLCD, temperature fitting) ✓ README.
- **Losses:** cross-entropy plus an energy penalty on benign data (Prompt Guard 2 reports this improves precision on out-of-distribution data, ✓ card) as a candidate; Brier or log loss on soft teacher labels for distillation; class weights chosen for the target low-FPR operating point.
- **Config and environment:** one config file per run (Hydra or plain YAML), `uv` lockfile, container image digest, seeds, and deterministic algorithm flags where available.

### 6.2 Evaluation suites and metrics

| Suite | Contents | Metrics |
|---|---|---|
| In-distribution | Held-out splits of the training mixture | AUROC, AUPRC, F1, TPR at 1% and 0.1% FPR (Prompt Guard 2 reports recall at 1% FPR, ✓ card) |
| External | JailbreakBench, HarmBench, XSTest, OR-Bench-hard, LLMail-Inject, held-out in-the-wild jailbreaks, ToxicChat (eval-only) | Same, per dataset, never pooled into one headline |
| Over-blocking | XSTest safe prompts, OR-Bench, JailbreakBench benign | FPR, by category |
| Slices | Language, length, chunk count, attack family, source, target model | Metric per slice with confidence intervals |
| Calibration | All of the above | ECE, Brier, reliability diagrams; float and integer models separately |
| Adaptive robustness | White-box attacks on the released weights; black-box LLM-guided search against the verdict; transfer from other detectors; chunk-boundary and encoding attacks; held-out attack families | Attack success rate at a fixed query budget, by attacker knowledge (white-box, black-box, hidden-weight mode C3) |
| Quantisation agreement | Float versus integer verdicts on every eval item; near-threshold slice | Flip count and flip rate; zero required between integer runtime and prover (spec) |
| Cost | Native latency per chunk; proving time, memory and proof size per chunk; verification time | Reported with hardware, prover version, security parameters and exclusions (spec § 13.4) |

Pin evaluators and versions (`meta/research-workflows.md` § Bounded research workflow): datasets by revision SHA, evaluator code by commit, models by `C_Ci`. Freeze the evaluation sets at M0 and treat repeated tuning against them as spending their value.

### 6.3 Calibration and threshold derivation

1. Fit temperature (or Platt scaling) on a held-out calibration split, on the integer model's logits.
2. Choose the operating point per policy (for example the threshold giving 1% FPR on benign calibration data, or a target precision); the choice is part of the policy and is reviewed, not tuned silently.
3. Convert to the integer log-odds threshold `t_i` at the quantised logit scale (spec § 6.2), and record both the float probability and the integer value in the release manifest.
4. Report the threshold's stability: bootstrap interval on FPR and TPR at `t_i`.

### 6.4 Quantisation and export to the circuit

? INFERRED pipeline; the principle "integer model is normative" is from the spec.

1. **Fix the target arithmetic first.** Read the chosen prover's fixed-point format (DeepProve 12-bit default, ZKTorch 10-bit, EZKL configurable scales; repo notes) and its approximations for softmax, GELU, LayerNorm and division.
2. **QAT to that arithmetic.** Insert fake-quant modules that mimic those approximations during the last training epochs, so accuracy is measured on the arithmetic that will be proven, not on bf16.
3. **Export.** ONNX with a pinned opset, static shapes (batch 1, sequence 512), eager attention (no flash-attention or unpadding paths), and the classification head ending in logits. Optimum or `torch.onnx.export`.
4. **Integer reference.** A small, readable integer implementation of the model (the "normative program") that the TEE runtime also executes for release gating.
5. **Agreement checks.** Integer reference versus prover witness generation on every evaluation item (bit-exact logits), and integer versus float verdicts (flip rate reported). Full proofs on a sample only: at the spec's 7.6 minutes per 512-token proof, 5,000 items would take about 633 server-hours (5,000 x 7.6 / 60), so constraint-checking (EZKL-style mock proving, where available) covers the rest. ✗ UNCERTAIN which provers offer a mock mode.
6. **Hand-off.** The verification key, integer model file, tokenizer, chunking rule, label map and scales go into `C_Ci`.

### 6.6 Experiment tracking and reproducibility

- Self-hosted [MLflow](https://github.com/mlflow/mlflow) (or local files) as the run tracker, with no hosted sync by default, as `meta/research-workflows.md` advises.

- Data versions in DVC or private Hub revisions; never mutate a released `D_v`.

## 7. Risks specific to the ML work

| Risk | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| "Passed" read as "safe" | Reputational damage | Every report says "ran as configured and returned pass"; robustness reported separately by attacker knowledge ([[nasr2025adaptive]]) |
| White-box attacks on public classifier weights | Attackers optimise against the exact proven model | Hidden-weight mode (spec C3), rotation of classifier versions, adversarial training, held-out attack families |
| Quantisation drift near threshold | Release and proof disagree | Integer model normative; QAT; near-threshold slice in agreement tests |
| Judge-label bias | Classifier learns the judge's blind spots | Multiple judge families, human audit, agreement bars |
| Licence contamination | NC data in a commercial model | Licence field in every record; training mixtures built only from cleared sources |
| Benchmark overfitting | Inflated metrics | Frozen eval sets, held-out families, external suites never trained on |
| Decision-system prompt sensitivity | Laya answers change with wording and option order (✓ README) | Committed fixed question prefix in `C_Ci`; order-averaging or parallel layout evaluated |
| Model too big to prove | M3 slips | Stage 1 sizes; distillation; sampling per spec |

## 8. Open questions

1. Which policies does the first relying party need (PRD question 1)? The data plan differs a lot between prompt-injection only and response harm.
2. Weight hiding: public classifier weights make white-box attacks easy; hidden weights cost more to prove. Which does the first sprint choose (PRD question 6)?
3. Which encoder operators do DeepProve, ZKTorch and EZKL support today, and at what cost per 512-token chunk (spec § 15 question 1)? This decides Stage 1's architecture.
4. Is a 300M to 400M encoder (Laya-sized) provable within a sampling budget on GPU provers, or does Laya stay tier T?
5. Licences: may Lucid train on Prompt Guard 2 (Llama 4 licence), on gated AI2 data, and on CC BY-NC sets? Who decides?
6. Can Jev be used as a labelling judge under TypeSafe's terms, and is that acceptable given data leaves to its service?
7. Multilingual scope: English only for the POC, or the Nemotron v3 languages?
8. Who writes and owns the policy rubric, and is it shared with the relying party as part of `policy_id` governance?
9. Should training provenance (`R_train`) be TEE-attested in the first build, or only signed?
10. What agreement bar between judges and humans makes synthetic labels acceptable, and how large must the human audit be?
